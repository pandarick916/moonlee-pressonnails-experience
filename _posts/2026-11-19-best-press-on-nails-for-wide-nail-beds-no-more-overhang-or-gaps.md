For years I assumed my nails were the problem. Every set of press-ons I bought left a visible strip of my own nail showing along both sides. Not a huge gap, just enough that anyone looking at my hands could tell. I sized up. The nail then sat on my skin at the sidewalls, caught on my sweater, and lifted by day two.

It took me an embarrassingly long time to work out that my nails were fine and the sizing system was the problem. Here's what actually fixed it. Two things, both boring.

## Side gaps: check width and curve separately

A press-on can have the length you want without covering your nail from sidewall to sidewall. Measure the width at the widest part of your natural nail, then compare the press-on's curve with your own. A gap alone doesn't tell you which measurement is wrong.

**Quick answer: why do press-on nails leave gaps at the sides?** The tip may be too narrow, or its curve may not match your nail. Try sizes without adhesive first. Look for sidewall coverage without pressing the tip flat or letting it rest on the surrounding skin.

## Choose the base fit before the tip shape

Square, squoval, round, and oval are useful shapes to compare, but the shape name doesn't tell you the width or curve at the base. Almond, coffin, and stiletto tips aren't automatically unsuitable for wide nail beds either. Look at the actual measurements and where the taper starts on the particular tip.

## Size count matters, but read it correctly

More sizes can offer smaller steps when they cover the same width range. They don't guarantee that: a brand might extend the range instead, or use uneven spacing. Read its actual size chart rather than treating the number of pieces as the number of distinct sizes.

Here's a hypothetical example, not a product specification. Ten evenly spaced widths from 8 to 16mm have nine intervals, each about 0.89mm. Sixteen widths over the same range have fifteen intervals, each about 0.53mm. For a 15.4mm measurement, the neighboring widths would be about 15.11 and 16mm in the first example. The second gives 14.93 and 15.47mm. Neither calculation proves a tip will fit. Curve and measurement method still matter.

## Material isn't a substitute for fit

Soft gel and ABS labels alone don't tell you whether a tip will fit. Weigh its width, curve, and cuticle edge instead of assuming one material will solve all three.

**Quick answer: is soft gel or ABS better for wide nail beds?** Neither is an automatic choice. Allure's [press-on nail roundup [1]](https://www.allure.com/gallery/best-press-on-nails) offers product comparisons. A review isn't a substitute for checking the size chart and trying a tip against your own nail.

Width is different from transverse curve, the arch across your nail. If a tip needs pressure to sit against your nail, investigate the curve as well as the width. ## Check before filing the sides

Filing can't add width to a tip that's too narrow. That is the whole rule. If a tip is slightly too wide, check whether its maker allows minor side shaping before you alter it. Avoid heavy filing that leaves the edge thin, uneven, or cracked.

Shortening the free edge may also be an option when the product instructions allow it. Instructions for one material, finish, or embellished design apply to every set.

## If your nail beds are small instead

The same logic runs in reverse, and the failure looks different. On small nail beds the tip doesn't fall short. It covers skin on both sides, lifts at the sidewall, and catches on a sweater sleeve before lunch. If you've been looking for the best press on nails for small nail beds and every set starts at a medium, that's the problem, not your application.

What to look for: a set that publishes its smallest width in millimetres, and one that goes below 10 mm. A per-finger chart beats a size count, because ten sizes that start at 11 mm still leave a small nail bed with nothing to wear. Filing a wider tip down works on the sides only, and only when the curve already matches. Narrow the tip and keep the wrong curve, and you've traded an overhang for a gap.

## Application notes for wide beds

Start with a dry fit. Adhesive shouldn't be used to compensate for a tip that's too narrow or needs force to sit comfortably.

- **Follow the adhesive's own directions.** Glue and adhesive tabs need different handling. Apply the specified amount or tab size and keep adhesive off the surrounding skin.
- **Follow the recommended placement and hold time.** A wider nail doesn't always need more adhesive or a longer hold.
- **Don't cut your cuticles.** The American Academy of Dermatology advises against cutting them because they help protect against infection.[1](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/nail-care-secrets/basics/pedicures/reduce-artificial-nail-damage)
- **Don't keep wearing a painful set.** If you notice pain or irritation, stop use and follow the product's removal instructions rather than forcing the tip off.

## FAQ

### Why do press-on nails leave gaps at the sides of my nails?

The tip may be too narrow, or its curve may not match your nail. Judge both during a dry fit. Never force the tip flat or let it rest on surrounding skin.

### What nail shape is best for wide nail beds?

No shape name guarantees a fit. Read the width and curve at the base of the specific tip. Square, squoval, round, and oval are options to try, not a reason to rule out every tapered design.

### Can I file press-on nails narrower to fit wide nails?

Filing can't fix a tip that's too narrow. If it's slightly too wide, check whether its maker allows minor side shaping. Avoid heavy filing that leaves a thin or damaged edge.

### Is soft gel better than ABS plastic for wide nail beds?

Not automatically. Verify the actual width, base curve, and cuticle edge instead of choosing by material alone.

### How many sizes should a press-on set have for wide nails?

There's no universal minimum. Note the actual widths and maximum size. More distinct sizes can mean smaller steps over the same range, but brands may use different ranges or uneven spacing.
