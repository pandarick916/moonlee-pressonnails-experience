For years I assumed my nails were the problem. Every set of press-ons I bought left a visible strip of my own nail showing along both sides — not a huge gap, just enough that anyone looking at my hands could tell. I sized up. The nail then sat on my skin at the sidewalls, caught on my sweater, and lifted by day two.

It took me an embarrassingly long time to work out that my nails were fine and the sizing system was the problem. Here is what actually fixed it.

## The gap at the sides is a width problem, not a you problem

Press-on sets are built around an assumed relationship between nail width and nail length. If your nail beds run wider than that assumption — which is extremely common on thumbs and index fingers — a nail that matches your width will look stubby, and a nail that matches your length will leave side gaps.

**Quick answer — why do press-on nails leave gaps at the sides?** Because sets are sized on a single width-to-length ratio. A wide nail bed matching that set's length will be 1 to 2 millimeters narrower than your actual nail, leaving bare sidewalls. Sizing up fixes width but overhangs skin, which causes lifting within 48 hours.

Once you see it as a ratio problem rather than a size problem, the fix becomes obvious: you need either a set with enough size granularity to break the ratio, or a shape whose geometry is naturally wider at the base.

## Shape does more work than anything else

This is the highest-leverage change, and it costs nothing.

**Shapes that suit wide nail beds:** square, squoval, round, and oval. All four hold their width from the cuticle through most of the nail before any taper begins. They cover the nail bed surface you actually have.

**Shapes that fight you:** stiletto, coffin, and any sharply tapered almond. These begin narrowing close to the base. On a wide bed, that taper starts inside your nail bed rather than past your fingertip, which is what produces the side gap — and no amount of adhesive closes a geometric gap.

Squoval is what I wear most. A true square looks correct on a wide nail but the sharp corners take a beating; the softened corner of a squoval survives daily use far better while keeping the same base width.

If shape selection is new to you, I worked through how each one sits against the cuticle line and sidewalls in [the guide to why press-ons look fake](https://moonleehome.com/blogs/experience-with-press-on-nails/why-your-press-on-nails-look-fake-the-cuticle-fit-sidewall-coverage-and-shape-guide-to-making-them-look-real). Sidewall coverage is the single strongest tell, and it is worse on wide beds than anywhere else.

## Size count matters, but read it correctly

A lot of advice says to buy sets with more sizes. That is right, but the reason usually given is wrong.

More sizes do not help because you get more nails. They help because they narrow the step between consecutive sizes. A set spanning 8mm to 16mm in ten sizes steps roughly 0.9mm at a time. The same span in sixteen sizes steps about 0.5mm. If your thumb measures 15.4mm, the first set makes you choose between 15.0 and 15.9 — one leaves a gap, the other sits on skin. The second set has something at 15.5.

## Material: the popular advice is half wrong

You will read everywhere that soft gel is always better than hard plastic for difficult nail beds, because it flexes to conform. The flex part is true. The "always" part is not.

**Quick answer — is soft gel or ABS better for wide nail beds?** Neither wins automatically. Allure's 2026 press-on review named an ABS plastic set its Best Overall at $20, praising it for sitting flush because it tapers toward the cuticle.[1](https://www.allure.com/gallery/best-press-on-nails) Cuticle-edge geometry decides fit on a wide bed, not material.

What flexibility genuinely helps with is transverse curve — the arch across your nail. That is a different axis from width, and if that is your issue you want [the flat nail bed and C-curve guide](https://moonleehome.com/blogs/experience-with-press-on-nails/stop-the-c-curve-torture-pain-flat-nail-beds) instead. Plenty of people have both, and it is worth knowing which one is causing which symptom: width problems show as side gaps, curve problems show as a lifting center.

My honest position: buy for size range and cuticle-edge shape first, treat material as a tiebreaker.

## Do not file the sides down

This comes up constantly and I understand the logic — the nail is too wide at the tip, so narrow it. It does not work.

Filing the sidewalls thins the part of the nail engineered to distribute pressure, which creates a stress point that cracks under normal use. On most press-ons there also is not enough material at the sidewall to remove meaningfully; you will compromise the structure before you gain a visible millimeter.

Filing the **free edge** — the tip, shortening the length — is completely fine and I do it on every set. It is only the sides that are structural.

## The wide thumb problem specifically

Thumbs are where wide nail beds cause the most trouble, because the gap between your thumb and your index finger is bigger than the gap between any other two adjacent sizes on your hand.

Two things that helped me:

1. **Buy two sets in adjacent sizes if your thumbs are the outlier.** Unglamorous, but a set is not expensive and you get the correct thumb plus a full spare set of everything else.
2. **Check whether your thumbs are wide or just flat.** Mine turned out to be both, and I spent a year solving the wrong one. ## Application notes for wide beds

Wide nails have more surface area, which is an advantage for bond strength and a liability for lifting, because a longer sidewall perimeter means more edge to fail along.

- **Apply adhesive across the full width,** not a dot in the center. Centre-dot application is the most common cause of sidewall lift on wide nails.
- **Press from the center outward** so air escapes toward the edges rather than getting trapped at one side.
- **Hold for a full thirty seconds.** More surface area needs more contact time under pressure.
- **Do not cut your cuticles first.** The American Academy of Dermatology is clear that cuticles protect against infection and that trimming them lets bacteria in.[2](https://www.aad.org/public/skin-hair-nails/nail-care/artificial-nails) Push them back instead.

## What to look for when you buy

My checklist, in priority order: a published millimeter size range that reaches your widest nail; a size step of roughly 0.5mm rather than 1mm; square, squoval, round, or oval shape; and a cuticle edge that tapers rather than sitting blunt.

## FAQ

**Why do press-on nails leave gaps at the sides of my nails?**
Because sets are sized on one width-to-length ratio. Sizing up fixes the width but overhangs skin, which causes lifting within 48 hours.

**What nail shape is best for wide nail beds?**
Square, squoval, round, and oval. Avoid stiletto, coffin, and sharply tapered almond.

**Can I file press-on nails narrower to fit wide nails?**
No. Filing the sidewalls creates a stress point that cracks under normal use. Filing the free edge to shorten length is fine.

**Is soft gel better than ABS plastic for wide nail beds?**
Not automatically. Prioritise size range and cuticle-edge shape, treat material as a tiebreaker.

**How many sizes should a press-on set have for wide nails?**
Look at the millimeter step. A set spanning 8–16mm in ten sizes steps ~0.9mm; sixteen sizes steps ~0.5mm.
