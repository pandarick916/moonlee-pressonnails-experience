Two weeks ago my sister-in-law pressed a soft gel tip onto her ring finger, held it ten seconds, and it stayed put. Then I handed her one of mine (same width on the ruler, same shape name on the box) and it clicked down at the center, floated at both edges, and popped off before we made it to the car.

That minute is the entire soft-versus-hard conversation. It's also why most of what you read about it online doesn't help you pick. The box is part of the problem too. The same M measures 12.38 mm in one of our shapes and 12.90 mm in another, so the letter you bought tells you less about the fit than the shape you chose.

MOONLEE is a handmade press-on line cut in four measured sizes, and flat nail beds are the reason it exists. I also make acrylic tips. So I've an obvious reason to tell you material doesn't matter. It does. It just does less than the shape of your own nail does, and I'd rather show you where the line falls than pretend it isn't there.

## Soft gel vs acrylic: what actually decides the fit

Soft gel and acrylic differ in how much they bend, and that difference decides less about fit than your nail's width and curve do. The question people search is which material is better. The question that decides whether a set survives your Tuesday is how much forgiveness you need, and where on your own nail that forgiveness has to happen.

Here's the scene I hear about most. It's 7:40 on a Tuesday, you're three slides away from presenting, and you type one sentence. Your middle nail lifts at the sidewall with a small click against the key. You look down and there's a shadow along the edge. Not a chip, not a crack. A shadow.

That isn't a glue failure and it isn't because the tips are "hard." that's a [press on nails for flat nail beds](https://moonleehome.com/blogs/experience-with-press-on-nails/press-on-nails-for-flat-nail-beds) problem meeting a tip molded for a rounder nail. The material only decided how loud the complaint would be.

What made me write this down is how reliably the two questions get mixed up. Someone searches "soft gel vs acrylic," reads a comparison chart, buys the softer tip, and still has a gap along the sidewall, because that chart was never about their nail. Someone else searches "press on nails for flat nail beds," finds almost nothing useful, and quietly decides the problem is them. Both of them are asking about fit and shopping for material. That's why this piece exists.

## What the two materials actually do, in three tests

Three tests separate a soft gel tip from an acrylic one, and you can run all of them with one credit card and a tip you've already worn. It works whether you're holding a soft gel press on nail or a hard press on nail. The tests don't care what the box says.

![Three tests for telling a soft gel press-on from a hard acrylic one, using a credit card, your own nail and a tip you have already worn](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0651/2210/7463/files/press-on-material-four-tests.webp?v=1795075940)

Fig. 1 — Three tests, none of which need a shop. Test three is the one that predicts lifting.

**1. The bend test.** Pinch a tip between thumb and index finger and try to fold it. A soft gel tip flexes into a shallow U and springs back. A standard acrylic tip resists, then releases with a snap. That difference is real, and it's the only thing the word "soft" is describing.

**2. The straight-edge test on your own nail.** Lay a credit card across your nail at the widest point, sidewall to sidewall, and sight along the top of the card from the fingertip end. What you're reading is the light, not the card. On a curved nail the card touches only the crest and you'll see two thin slivers of light at the sidewalls. On a flat nail it lies across the full width and you'll see no sliver at either side. One sliver on one side only means the nail is asymmetric, which is common and is why you size each hand separately. That's the whole **flat vs curved nail bed** question, and a credit card reads easier than a ruler because it's wider than your nail. Do it on both hands. They won't match. If you land on the curved side and want the long version, I wrote about [why a strong C-curve fights a flat bed](https://moonleehome.com/blogs/experience-with-press-on-nails/stop-the-c-curve-torture-pain-flat-nail-beds) separately.

**3. The last-set test.** Pick up a tip you've already worn and look at the underside, or at the adhesive it left on your nail. Residue spread across the full width means contact was even. Residue only down the middle, with the two outer thirds clean, means the tip was more arched than the nail it sat on. That's the pattern that lifts first at the sidewall, and you're not guessing at it. You already ran the experiment.

👉 If the residue only held the middle third, the material was never your problem. The arch was.

## Gel-X, soft gel, hard gel — why the names trip everyone up

Gel-X is a brand name, soft gel describes the plastic, and hard acrylic describes how little it bends. Half the confusion in this category is vocabulary, so I'll take the words apart before they cost you money.

![Gel-X, soft gel and hard acrylic compared: what each word describes and what it leaves out](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0651/2210/7463/files/gel-x-soft-gel-acrylic-names.webp?v=1795073354)

Fig. 2 — Three words, two different questions. Only one of them is about attachment.

**Gel-X** is a brand name, not a material. It belongs to Aprés, and it describes a soft gel extension tip that gets adhered with a gel adhesive and cured under a lamp. When people search **gel x vs press ons**, they're usually comparing two things that are built the same way — a full-cover tip over your natural nail — and differ in how the tip is attached and cured. That's why the comparison feels slippery. It isn't really a material question. It's an attachment question, and the fit question sits underneath both.

**Soft gel** describes the tip itself: a thinner, more flexible full-cover nail that bends under finger pressure. A **soft gel press on nail** can be worn with tabs or with glue; the word tells you about the plastic, not the adhesive.

**Hard acrylic press on nails** (what I make) are stiffer. They hold a molded curve instead of bending to yours. That's the trade: more shape control and a cleaner free edge, less forgiveness when the curve is wrong.

The short version of the naming lives in the glossary, under [press-on nails vs Gel-X](https://moonleehome.com/pages/nail-glossary/press-on-vs-gel-x). For the rest of this piece, "soft gel" means the flexible tip and "hard" means acrylic, and I'll use them that way.

## Why the gap always shows up at the sidewalls

A nail plate is curved in two directions at once, and the side-to-side arch is the one that ruins press-ons. It also varies more than people expect. Researchers measured the transverse curvature of 182 adults with radius gauges and found the thumbnail is the flattest of the five digits, with the little finger the most curved[1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21615423/). The same study found nails are flatter in the dominant hand, in men, in older people, and in people with wider hands.

That's a group average, not a reading of your hands. It explains a complaint I hear constantly, "it only lifts on my right hand," without telling you which of your hands is which. Plenty of people have the flatter nail on the non-dominant side. The study moves the odds, it doesn't decide the case. What holds either way: the tip didn't change between your hands. Your nail did.

![Cross-section of the same press-on tip on two nails: one whose arch matches the tip with even contact, and one with a steeper arch where the tip lifts along both sidewalls](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0651/2210/7463/files/press-on-sidewall-gap-cross-section.webp?v=1795073406)

Fig. 3 — Same tip in both panels. The left nail matches its arch; the right nail's arch is steeper, so the tip lifts along both sidewalls.

When the tip's arch and your nail's don't line up, contact drops to a narrow strip and both sidewalls stay open. A moulded tip only comes in one arch, so the mismatch almost always lands on your side, not the tip's. Two things follow. First, the adhesive only holds that strip, so any sideways force — a zipper, a seatbelt, a keyboard — peels from the free edge inward, because the edge has nothing under it. Second, the gap is a pocket. It holds water after you wash your hands, and a damp pocket under an artificial nail is how [green nail syndrome](https://moonleehome.com/blogs/experience-with-press-on-nails/straight-up-guide-press-on-nails-greenies-pseudomonas) gets started. That one is bacterial, not dirt and not mould, and I wrote a separate piece on it because it's the one outcome I wouldn't wish on anyone.

Shoppers describe this better than any product page does. One line from a thread we archived in our Reddit research: **"The flat nail bed thing is real. The olive and June ones are SUPER curvy, and pop off really quickly."** u/Zarine420, r/Nails[2](https://www.reddit.com/r/Nails/). Read what she's describing. The tip didn't fail to stick. It was curvier than her nail, so it popped. We didn't save the thread link when we archived it, so take that as one shopper's account rather than a citation.

## The number that explains most "wrong size" complaints

Inside one brand, the same size letter isn't the same width across shapes, and that one fact explains most "wrong size" complaints. You can check it without buying anything. Our size chart sorts you into four bands by width, and each shape is then moulded separately inside that band. These are the widths the moulds actually come out at:

| Shape | XS | S | M | L  ---  ---  ---  Short Oval | 10.70 | 11.80 | 12.87 | 13.20  Short Squoval | 10.80 | 11.85 | 12.70 | 13.50  Short Square | 10.95 | 11.36 | 12.38 | 13.40  Short Almond | 11.50 | 12.00 | 12.75 | 13.50  Medium Almond | 11.34 | 12.36 | 12.90 | 13.78 |

Two numbers are doing two different jobs here, and mixing them up is where most sizing goes wrong. The bands tell you which letter to start with. The mould widths tell you what that letter measures in the shape you're actually buying.

An M is 12.38 mm on a Short Square and 12.90 mm on a Medium Almond. Half a millimeter sounds like nothing until you remember that a press-on either covers your sidewalls or it doesn't — there's no "close." If you bought an M last time and it floated, the letter wasn't wrong. The shape was.

This is also the part a bend test can't save you from. A flexible tip that's 0.5 mm narrow will still lift at the corners. It just lifts while feeling soft. Softness buys tolerance, not width.

## The four steps that decide whether a hard set gaps

I wear acrylic on a flat nail bed. Four things decide whether it holds: a dry fit, the preparation the adhesive asks for, the package's own sequence, and no extra pressure. Each step has a reason, and the reason is the part that matters.

![Four application steps for hard press-on nails: alcohol wipe, dry-fit, place at an angle, then stop pressing harder](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0651/2210/7463/files/hard-press-on-application-four-steps.webp?v=1795075949)

Fig. 5 — The order carries more weight than the products. Step four is the one people skip.

**Step 1: Wipe the nail with alcohol and wait thirty seconds.** Not a rinse, not a wipe with lotion on your hands. Seventy percent alcohol on a cotton pad, over the whole plate, then let it evaporate. Adhesive doesn't bond to oil, and oil comes back within minutes of any hand cream. Most "the glue is bad" verdicts are oil verdicts.

**Step 2: Dry-fit before you open any adhesive.** Press the tip on with nothing on it and look for light at both sidewalls. If you see a shadow on one side only, rotate through the other tips of that size before going up a size — nail beds aren't symmetric. If both sides shadow, the size is right and the profile is wrong; go shorter or switch to a flatter shape. That profile question is what I call [apex, and it's the reason most sets lift](https://moonleehome.com/blogs/experience-with-press-on-nails/apex-press-on-nails-why-they-lift) even when the width is correct. This one step is why I stopped wasting sets.

**Step 3: Place at an angle, then press from cuticle to tip.** Set the cuticle edge first at about 45 degrees, roll down, and hold ten seconds with steady pressure. Don't try to squeeze the sidewalls in afterward. Acrylic doesn't reshape at room temperature, so the pinch buys you nothing but a stressed tip. If a corner still won't seat, that's a width or profile answer, not a pressure answer.

**Step 4: Stop pressing harder.** Pressure doesn't close a curve gap. It squeezes adhesive out of the contact strip you do have and leaves the gap exactly where it was, now drier. If a tip won't sit, the answer is a different tip, not more force.

Maybe you have already tried the hot-water trick to flatten a stubborn curve. It does work up to a point — I tested it and wrote about [where flattening a press-on stops working](https://moonleehome.com/blogs/experience-with-press-on-nails/flatten-press-on-nails-hot-water), including the part that quietly ruins the tip.

## When I tell people to skip acrylic entirely

If your nail has a pronounced arch, a pre-shaped acrylic tip will fight you on every finger, and I'd rather say that than sell you one. There's a version of this where I hand you a discount code and tell you hard tips work for everyone. I'm not going to write that one.

If your nail has a pronounced arch (you can see it from the side without a ruler, or the sides tuck under like a pincer), a pre-shaped acrylic tip will fight you on every finger. The American Academy of Dermatology notes that gel nails are more flexible than acrylic[3](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/nail-care-secrets/basics/pedicures/reduce-artificial-nail-damage). That guidance is about salon applications and nail damage, not about pre-shaped tips fitting one specific curve, so I'll only take it this far: a more flexible tip has more room to give. If your arch is strong, start with the softer material. I'd rather you wear something that fits than something from my shop that doesn't.

![Decision chart for choosing between soft gel and acrylic press-on nails based on the arch of your nail](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0651/2210/7463/files/which-press-on-material-decision.webp?v=1795075960)

Fig. 6 — Which material, for which nail. One ten-second test decides it.

Two more things I won't pretend away. Any artificial nail, worn without a break, is hard on the plate, and the same AAD guidance suggests keeping them for occasions rather than continuous wear[3](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/nail-care-secrets/basics/pedicures/reduce-artificial-nail-damage). And don't use a set to cover a nail that's already lifting, peeling, or painful. Cover it and you trap whatever is going on underneath.

What I'll argue with is the idea that flat nail beds are stuck. They aren't. They are badly served by tips sold in one default curve, in sizes guessed from a photo. Width you can measure in millimeters. Curve you can check with a credit card in ten seconds. Neither one requires you to change materials — it requires you to stop buying the shape that was never yours. If you want the version of this that's about packing instead of geometry, I take [Ivory Pudding short almonds on airport days](https://moonleehome.com/blogs/experience-with-press-on-nails/press-on-nails-for-travel-vacation-how-to-pack-apply-make-them-last-your-entire-trip) for exactly that reason: cuffs, tray tables, and one less thing to think about.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Gel-X the same thing as soft gel press on nails?

Almost, and the difference is in how they attach. Gel-X is a brand line of soft gel extension tips that cure under a lamp with a gel adhesive. A soft gel press on nail is the same idea sold with tabs or brush-on glue, no lamp needed. The plastic is close to identical. The curing step is the fork in the road. If your goal is a set you can remove in warm water on a Sunday, tabs win. If you want three weeks and you're set up for it (a lamp, a gel adhesive, and a base coat compatible with the tips you bought), the cured route holds longer. A lamp on its own doesn't get you there.

### Are soft gel press-ons better for flat nail beds?

More forgiving, yes — a flexible tip bends toward your nail instead of bridging over it. But "better" only holds if the width is right. A soft gel tip that's 1.5 mm too narrow will lift at the sidewalls as fast as a hard one, and it'll feel like it fits while it does it. If your set is lifting for reasons other than the curve, [the other five causes are here](https://moonleehome.com/blogs/experience-with-press-on-nails/why-press-on-nails-fall-off).

### Do you make press-ons for wide or flat nail beds, and in what widths?

That's the whole reason the size chart is published in millimetres instead of letters. The bands start at 8 mm for XS and run past 13.5 mm for L, and then each shape is moulded on its own, so M alone spans 12.38 to 12.90 mm across our shapes. If you measure 13.4 mm across the widest point of your thumb, you're inside L for some shapes and right at the edge for others, and the chart tells you which before you pay.

### Can I make hard acrylic press on nails work on a flat nail?

Usually, if you change two things: go shorter, and dry-fit until both sidewalls disappear before you open any adhesive. What you can't do is press harder, add more glue, or pinch the corners in. None of the three closes a curve gap.

### Why do my press-ons only lift on one hand?

Because your two hands aren't the same shape. Transverse nail curvature is flatter in the dominant hand on average[1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21615423/), so the same tip can fit one hand and bridge the other. Which hand that's varies by person, so measure both instead of assuming.

### Does soft gel wear shorter than acrylic?

Not because of the material, in my experience — because of the thickness. A thinner tip flexes more, and flex at the free edge is what eventually lifts an edge. Prep and adhesive choice move wear time more than the label on the box does. Two pieces go deeper on that: [glue vs tabs over 21 days](https://moonleehome.com/blogs/experience-with-press-on-nails/press-on-nail-glue-vs-tabs-21-day-wear-myth), and [how long it's actually safe to keep a set on](https://moonleehome.com/blogs/experience-with-press-on-nails/how-long-is-it-safe-to-wear-press-on-nails-dermatologist-answers).
