My cousin's wedding was on a Saturday in June, and I spent the Tuesday before it staring at two open carts. One had a $14.99 machine-made set in it. The other had a hand-painted set at $26.99, and I could not have told you what the extra twelve dollars was buying.

I bought the hand-painted one. Then I made the mistake that costs more than the nails do.

## What Kept Happening Every Time I Bought Handmade

I picked S, because S is what I have always called myself, and I measured nothing.

In the box they were beautiful. On my hands, the thumb sat about a millimeter wide of my nail bed on both sides — close enough that I told myself it was fine and glued it down anyway. At the rehearsal dinner I reached across the table for the bread basket, the outer corner of that thumb caught the hem of a linen napkin, and I heard the small click of it letting go. Press-on nails lifting at the edges is annoying on a Tuesday. On the night before a wedding, with a set I had paid double for, I ate the rest of my dinner with my right hand in my lap.

So I had spent $26.99 and still could not answer the question I started with: what does handmade actually buy you, and how do you tell before your card gets charged?

## Why Handmade Sets Are So Hard to Judge

Two reasons, and neither one is about anybody being shady.

Handmade is a description, not a standard. Nobody certifies it. A set painted one petal at a time and a set printed to look painted can both carry the word, and product photos will not separate them for you.

The second reason is production math. Hand-painting is slow, so handmade studios work in small batches, and every additional size in a batch means another run of blanks to paint by hand. That cost falls on a very short production run. Sizing — not artwork — is where handmade sets differ most from each other, and it is the first thing I check now.

Fit is also what decides whether your own nails come out of this fine. The American Academy of Dermatology's guidance on artificial nails keeps landing on the same point: the damage comes from prying, filing and force, not from the nail sitting there[1](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/nail-care-secrets/basics/pedicures/reduce-artificial-nail-damage). A tip that runs a millimeter wide of your nail bed hands the world a lever on your nail plate every time you catch it on a napkin, a car door, a laptop lid. The AAD's basic rules — keep the plate clean, dry, and don't use your nails as tools — exist because that plate does not grow back overnight[2](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/nail-care-secrets/basics/healthy-nail-tips).

### 1. Per-finger millimeters, not a letter

An S from one shop and an S from another are two different objects. Width is the only number that transfers, because it is the one your nail bed cares about.

Our smallest live width is on the Short Squoval XS: pinky 6.08mm, thumb 10.68mm. The widest is the Almond L — thumb 13.78mm, middle 11.58mm, pinky 9.00mm — with the Medium Oval L close behind at 13.45mm on the thumb and 9.31mm on the pinky. Fourteen shapes are measured in the database, 54 variants total, and the widths are complete for all of them.

The honest limit: XS is as small as we go. There is no XXS. If your pinky measures under 6mm you will be filing the sides down, and I would rather tell you that now than have you find out at a rehearsal dinner. Same for curve — if your nail plate is flat rather than domed, the width can be right and the set can still fight you, which I wrote about in [the flat nail bed guide](https://moonleehome.com/blogs/experience-with-press-on-nails/stop-the-c-curve-torture-pain-flat-nail-beds).

Measure at the widest point of each nail, one finger at a time, and write the ten numbers down. A paper strip and a ruler work. So does the [free 30-second sizing tool](https://moonleehome.com/pages/press-on-nail-size-chart?utm_source=blog&utm_medium=content&utm_campaign=luxury-handmade-press-on-nails-are-they-worth-it).

### 2. More than a single size range

When a design ships in one size range, somebody's hands are being rounded off. Usually it is the wide thumbs and the narrow pinkies — which is most of us on at least one finger.

Count the size options before you look at the art. Right now 28 of our 36 live handmade sets come in XS/S/M/L, and the remaining 8 come in XS/S/M. That is a manufacturing decision we pay for on every batch, and it is the single thing I would tell a small studio to fix first.

### 3. A price you can break apart

The gap should be explainable in hours, not in adjectives. A machine-made set here is $14.99. Handmade runs $19.99 to $35.00, and most of them sit at $24.99. The hand-painted and 3D pieces — the ones where somebody built a raised flower with a brush — are $26.99.

So the honest version of the pitch is: about ten to twelve dollars buys you the painting hours and the small-batch run. If a shop cannot show you both a machine-made price and a handmade price, you have nothing to compare the premium against, which is exactly the spot I was in with those two carts. If you want the longer version of how custom painting gets priced and ordered, I went through it in [the hand-painted ordering guide](https://moonleehome.com/blogs/experience-with-press-on-nails/custom-hand-painted-press-on-nails-why-reddit-cant-get-enough-and-how-to-order-your-first-set).

### 4. A return window in writing

A stated window is what separates a shop from a hobby. Ours is 14 days on US orders. On a $14.99 set a bad fit is a shrug; at $26.99 it is a real decision, and the window is what lets you make it without gambling.

Two of the four checks are about fit. That is not an accident. ## FAQ

### Are luxury handmade press-on nails worth the extra money?

For me, yes, once I stopped guessing at size. The math I use: machine-made sets here are $14.99, handmade runs $19.99 to $35.00 with most at $24.99, and the hand-painted and 3D work is $26.99. That is roughly a ten to twelve dollar premium, and it buys painting hours on a small batch. Split across three wears, my $26.99 set cost nine dollars a wear. If a set only survives one wear because the width was wrong, no premium is worth it.

### What is the real difference between handmade and machine-made press-on nails?

Three things I can actually point at. Art: hand-painted and 3D pieces are built with a brush, one nail at a time, which is where the $26.99 goes. Batch size: handmade runs are small, so designs sell out and come back rather than sitting in stock forever. Sizing: this is the one people miss — 28 of our 36 live handmade sets come in XS/S/M/L and 8 come in XS/S/M, and that size count matters more to your wear time than the artwork does.

### How do I size handmade press-ons for wide or small nail beds?

Read millimeters, not letters. Measure each nail at its widest point and compare against the published widths. Our narrowest live tip is the Short Squoval XS pinky at 6.08mm, with a 10.68mm thumb in the same set. Our widest is the Almond L: 13.78mm thumb, 11.58mm middle, 9.00mm pinky, with the Medium Oval L at 13.45mm thumb and 9.31mm pinky. Fourteen shapes and 54 size variants are measured. If your pinky comes in under 6mm, XS is our floor and you will need to file the sides down. If your nail plate is flat rather than curved, pick a flatter shape rather than a wider size.

### What happens if the set does not fit when it arrives?

US orders have a 14-day return and exchange window. I would rather you send back a set that gapped at the sidewalls than glue it down and lose a nail plate to it.

### How many times can I reuse a handmade set?

I get three to four wears out of a hand-painted set, and I have pushed one to five. What kills them is removal, not wear — soak the nail off in warm soapy water or acetone and let the glue give up on its own instead of levering the tip up, which is the same advice removal guides keep repeating[3](https://www.allure.com/story/how-to-remove-press-on-nails). Scrape the old glue off the inside with a wooden stick, store them in the tray they came in, and keep the 3D pieces away from anything that will crush them.

### For a wedding, should I buy a handmade set or book a nail tech?

Depends whose wedding it is. The gel sets I have booked ran $65 to $85 before tip, plus two hours in the chair and a second appointment for removal. A $26.99 hand-painted set is twenty minutes at my kitchen table the night before, and I can pack a backup nail in my clutch, which no salon can offer me. As a guest, press-ons win and it is not close. If it were my own wedding, I would book the tech and keep a handmade set as the backup — I want somebody else responsible for my hands that morning.
